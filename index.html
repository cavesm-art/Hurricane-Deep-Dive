How do we stay safe when the sky turns deadly?

Tornadoes strike with almost no warning and last minutes. Hurricanes are the opposite: they can be spotted and tracked across the open ocean for days or even weeks before they make landfall.

That lead time is one of the greatest life-saving advantages in all of weather science, but only if forecasters can accurately predict where a storm is going and how strong it will be when it arrives. Today we build a complete picture of how hurricanes work, and how scientists follow them every mile of the way. At the end, you’ll step into the forecaster’s chair with real data from real hurricanes.

Section 1 of 5

How hurricanes form, step by step

Hurricanes don’t just appear. They grow through a defined progression, and each stage needs specific conditions to advance to the next. The whole process is powered by one thing above all else: warm ocean water.

It starts with a tropical disturbance, a disorganized cluster of thunderstorms over warm tropical ocean water, usually where sea surface temperatures exceed 26°C (79°F). The warm water evaporates rapidly, loading the air with heat and moisture. That warm, moist air rises, cools, condenses into clouds and rain, and releases latent heat, which warms the surrounding air and drives more rising air in a self-reinforcing convection loop.

As the system organizes, Earth’s rotation causes the rising air to begin spinning: counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere due to the Coriolis effect. Once the system develops a defined circulation and sustained winds above 39 mph, it earns the name tropical storm and gets its first official name from a predetermined list. When winds reach 74 mph, it officially becomes a hurricane.

The most intense part of the storm is the eyewall, a ring of towering thunderstorms surrounding the calm, clear eye at the center. In the eye, air is actually sinking and pressure is at its absolute lowest. Surrounding the eyewall, spiral rainbands extend outward for hundreds of miles. A hurricane weakens rapidly when it moves over cooler water, makes landfall (cutting off the warm ocean energy source), or meets strong wind shear that disrupts its organized structure.

Hurricane data and patterns: where, when, and why

Hurricanes are not random. They follow predictable geographic and seasonal patterns driven by ocean temperature and atmospheric circulation.

In the Atlantic basin, hurricane season officially runs June 1 through November 30, with the peak of activity typically in mid-September. That timing is no coincidence: it lines up with when the tropical Atlantic reaches its warmest sea surface temperatures after months of summer heating.

A season, day by day

Real data: average number of named storms active on each day, Atlantic, 1966–2025.

Storm activity comes from NOAA’s HURDAT2 best-track database. The sunlight and ocean heat curves are simplified sketches of the timing, not measurements.

Most Atlantic hurricanes form in one of three areas: the Gulf of Mexico, the Caribbean Sea, or the main development region, a broad swath of the tropical Atlantic stretching from the West African coast to the Caribbean. Storms that form near Africa, sometimes called Cape Verde storms, travel the longest distances and have the most time over warm water to intensify, producing some of the most powerful Atlantic hurricanes on record.

On average the Atlantic produces about 14 named storms per season, of which roughly 7 become hurricanes and 3 become major hurricanes (Category 3 or higher). But activity varies a lot from year to year. A key factor is El Niño, a periodic warming of the Pacific Ocean that increases upper-level wind shear over the Atlantic, disrupting hurricane development and producing quieter seasons. La Niña years tend to produce more active Atlantic seasons.

Section 3 of 5

The Saffir-Simpson scale: measuring intensity

Hurricanes are rated on the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale, a Category 1–5 system based on sustained wind speed.

Unlike the EF Scale for tornadoes, where ratings are assigned after the fact from damage surveys, Saffir-Simpson ratings are based on measured wind speeds, collected in real time by hurricane hunter aircraft flying directly into the storm and by satellite analysis. One critical warning: the category tells you about wind, but wind is often not the deadliest part of a hurricane. Storm surge, the wall of ocean water pushed onshore, and inland flooding from rainfall account for the majority of hurricane deaths.

Section 4 of 5

Warning systems and staying safe

The National Hurricane Center issues a tiered alert system as a storm approaches. The key distinction is between a watch and a warning.

That difference determines whether people have 48 hours or 36 hours to prepare, and whether evacuation is still possible before conditions get worse.

⚠️ Hurricane watch

Hurricane conditions are possible within 48 hours. This is the time to prepare: gather supplies, review evacuation routes, and decide whether to stay or go. Don’t wait for a warning to start moving.

🚨 Hurricane warning

Hurricane conditions are expected within 36 hours. If local officials order evacuation, leave immediately. Roads may become impassable as conditions worsen and emergency services will not be available during the storm.

No alerts yetThe storm is far out at sea.

    The deadliest threat during most hurricanes is storm surge: the abnormal rise in sea level caused by the storm’s winds pushing water onshore. A major hurricane can raise water levels 15–20 feet above normal in the most exposed coastal areas. Storm surge arrives quickly, moves powerfully, and can push miles inland in low-lying areas. People who ignore evacuation orders in storm surge zones face life-threatening risk.

    For people who do shelter in place, the safest approach is to stay in an interior room, have 72 hours of supplies, and, critically, do not go outside during the eye passage. The temporary calm when the eye passes overhead is not the end of the storm. The back half of the eyewall arrives shortly after, and can be just as violent as the first half.

    How scientists track and predict hurricane paths

    One of the greatest life-saving advantages scientists have with hurricanes is time. A typical Atlantic hurricane can be detected and continuously tracked for 7–10 days before making landfall.

    That lead time is only possible because of a network of observation tools working together around the clock.

    The observing network

    Tap each tool in the scene.

    All of this data feeds into computer forecast models: complex mathematical equations that simulate the atmosphere and project where a storm will move and how it will change. Forecasters run many different models at once, each using slightly different equations and assumptions. When most models agree on a track, forecasters have high confidence. When models disagree and “spaghetti plots” fan out in different directions, uncertainty is high.

    The National Hurricane Center combines model guidance with forecaster expertise to produce the official forecast track, shown as the familiar cone of uncertainty: the funnel-shaped graphic showing the probable path of the storm’s center over the next five days.

    Spaghetti and the cone

    Change how much the models agree. Then turn on the storm’s wind field.

    Show

    A critical and widely misunderstood feature of the cone is what it does not show. The cone represents where the storm’s center is likely to travel. It does not mean conditions are only dangerous inside the cone. Hurricanes produce hazardous winds, storm surge, and rainfall hundreds of miles from the center. A community outside the cone can still experience major hurricane conditions if the storm wobbles slightly or if the storm’s rainbands extend far from the track.

    The naming system reinforces public awareness: a named storm gets media coverage that drives preparation in a way a technical code never could. Names of especially deadly or destructive storms are retired permanently. Katrina, Harvey, Maria, Sandy, and Ida are among dozens of retired names that will never be used again.
